# Product

## Register

product

## Users

People who attend live events — concerts, sports, festivals — and want a permanent, personal archive of those experiences. They care about the memory, not the logistics. They may be obsessive gig-goers with hundreds of shows, casual fans who want to remember a few big nights, or sports followers tracking seasons. They open the app after the event, not before. Mobile-first, likely using it in quiet moments of reflection.

## Product Purpose

TicketBook is a personal digital ticket stub archive. Users log events they've attended, building a collection of stubs that captures who was there, what the score was, and how good it was. The product exists to hold memories, not manage tickets. Success looks like an archive that feels worth keeping — something a user would show someone like they'd pull out a box of old stubs.

## Brand Personality

Deliberate, tactile, quietly warm. Like a well-kept physical collection — a shelf of vinyl or a box of ticket stubs — where every item has weight. There's warmth from the shared-memory layer (friends who were there, attendees), but the core is personal and archival, not social-performative. Confident without announcing itself.

## Anti-references

- **Spotify / streaming apps**: green-on-black, rounded album thumbnails, algorithmic-smooth surfaces. Too commercial and content-feed.
- **Ticketmaster / Dice**: functional purchase flows, event discovery UI. TicketBook is retrospective memory, not forward-facing ticketing.
- **Generic dark SaaS**: gradient cards, glassmorphism, hero metrics, "AI-made" aesthetic. No performative modernity.
- **Social fitness apps** (Strava, Garmin): leaderboard-first, gamification as the main loop. Stats exist here to reflect the collection, not to compete.

## Design Principles

1. **Artifacts over metrics** — an event stub is a meaningful object, not a score. Design surfaces it with that weight: typography, structure, and hierarchy that treats each entry as something worth keeping.
2. **Quiet authority** — no celebration animations for empty states, no nudge copy, no urgency. The product trusts users know why they're here. Restraint is respect.
3. **Tactile legibility** — the ticket-stub card metaphor is structural, not decorative. Physical ticket language (notches, stub numbers, perforations, venue/date/city layout) earns its place. Nothing should feel like it was skinned to look like a ticket.
4. **Warmth in the details** — the social layer (friends, who attended) should feel present without dominating. The collection is personal first, shared second.
5. **Data as memory, not dashboard** — stats and rankings exist to reflect back the archive, not to generate engagement. The numbers should feel like facts in a notebook, not leaderboard callouts.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

WCAG AA. Ensure sufficient contrast on all text against dark backgrounds. No reliance on color alone to convey event type (icons + labels always paired with color). Support Dynamic Type on iOS where feasible.
